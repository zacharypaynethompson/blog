I went to Nesta's [Policy Live](https://www.nesta.org.uk/feature/policy-live-2026/) on Thursday and I found the talks of the day very interesting and highly relevant to my work. The framing for the day was four shifts: how we're governed, how we relate to each other, our place in the world, and the technology underpinning this. Below are some of my thoughts from the sessions I attended.

## The shifts reshaping the UK

_Morning keynote with James Kanagasooriam (Focaldata) and Elisabeth Costa (Nesta), on the long-term shifts the UK needs to grapple with to be successful by 2040, and what they mean for politics and policy._

The opening keynote covered drivers of public pessimism and what might counteract them, with the suggested levers being a sense of duty, dynamism, agency, civic partnership, and moving away from viewing the world as zero-sum. The polling behind it was interesting, particularly the finding that optimism tracks perceived trajectory rather than current circumstances, so people can be doing badly and still be positive if they think things are improving.

My question is how you would ever isolate any of this. These are all correlated with each other and with the obvious confounders, so it's hard to know which of the five variables is explaining the most, or whether they're all downstream of something else that polling can't measure...

## Loneliness and information silos

_"Fractured realities", with Olivia Laing (author), Daniel Gorman and Heather Stewart. On loneliness as a civic and political problem rather than only a public health one, and what it would take to rebuild spaces of genuine connection when so much interaction is mediated by digital platforms._

This session was mostly qualitative and there wasn't much quantitative grounding for the claims about scale or mechanism, but it was interesting nonetheless to be in a space where this was an appreciated policy priority.

One main gap in the discussion, I felt, was that the consequences of loneliness that are most acute come out of the male loneliness crisis, e.g., violence against women and girls, and racialised violence emerging from the online right, weren't connected to the policy solutions being discussed. The solutions were mostly about broad civic participation, which I do think is right in principle, in the sense that a functioning society needs the incidental contact that comes from going to the post office or meeting other parents when taking a baby to a hospital appointment. But the route from "more libraries" to "fewer radicalised young men" is long and slightly tenuous, especially without evidence.

The men most at risk here will simply not go to a library. So the intervention has to happen in the spaces they already use, and I'd have liked the panel to engage with sports clubs, gyms and similar as sites for this rather than treating physical space generically. There was also very little on what the experience of being that man is actually like in a modern digital world, e.g., aspects of shame, lack of self-worth, and how isolation manifests for them.

The online regulation discussion was better, particularly the framing of freedom of speech within a human rights framework rather than as an absolute, which gives you something to actually enforce against algorithmic amplification.

## The UK's new place in the world

_"The middle child", with Mark Leonard, Anand Menon, Polly Mackenzie, James Crabtree and Olivia O'Sullivan. On what role the UK can have globally as a middle power, as the rules-based order gives way to great power rivalry, and the domestic consequences of that instability._

Refreshing in that it covered both soft and hard power, including gastrodiplomacy programmes in Korea. Korean cultural export has been a sustained central government objective with real resource behind it, and you can see the results in K-pop and Korean food.

I asked the panel how soft power actually connects to hard power and didn't get a clear answer. I spoke afterwards to someone working with the soft power council at No. 10 who had a more useful version, which is that the Cool Britannia era never really made that link, whereas comparators like France, Germany, the Netherlands and South Korea now use cultural leverage to work around genuine hard power constraints. Ireland is perhaps the sharpest example, converting diaspora and cultural identity into a level of diplomatic access, e.g., the annual St Patrick's Day meeting at the White House, that no comparable state has.

The panel also touched on, as opposed to other nations, that the UK has a specific problem here, which is a discomfort, mostly on the left, about associating soft power with imperial legacy. Many things in British culture have some colonial connection (down to a coronation chicken sandwich!) but none of us were alive for any of it. I can see the same reluctance affecting cultural support for e.g., British Black culture, which is one of our most successful cultural exports, but often still gets treated as somehow separate from British culture rather than as it.

## Getting good growth in every postcode

_Treasury Minister Dan Tomlinson MP in conversation with Tim Leunig (Nesta Chief Economist), on the pledge to achieve good growth in every postcode and what it would take in practice, given England's large cities outside London underperform their international counterparts._

There was little plan on data, which is a recurring theme across the day. The ideas on local agency and innovation were good, but the session was much more about getting _some_ growth than about what makes growth _good_, despite "good growth" being the stated framing.

One thing that was interesting, was that the minister accepted devolution meant that some areas will fail. The treasury minister on the panel, Dan Tomlinson, did seem content with the fact that a true devolution of powers would not, in the short term, mean there was even _growth_ in every postcode, let alone "good" growth.

## The evolution of devolution

_With Ed Richards, Mike Emmerich, Tim Leunig, Kirsty McLean and Anthony Breach, on the opportunities and risks of expanding devolved policymaking, following the plans to grant mayors a share of local income tax and business rates revenues._

This was the most substantive session of the afternoon. Some points I took away were that: an economy-first logic in England has been more successful than the Scottish model; devolution alone isn't sufficient and the panel were clear about needing to specify what else has to come with it; and fiscal devolution needs to go further to fix the incentives facing mayors.

The data question came up again, i.e., everyone agrees we need it, nobody was clear on how. A "what works" centre for devolved policy was floated, which seems like the right shape of answer, though it only works if the devolved bodies are actually generating comparable data in the first place, which I think is a massive technical challenge.

As always, Tim Leunig was very engaging and was one of the few panelists throughout the day to repeatedly hammer home the importance of data in successful policy making. In this session he suggested acts of primary legislation that enforced devolved bodies to provide adequate data on their policy implementations as a pre-requisite of their new powers.

---

Overall the day was engaging and helpful in situating the policy work we contribute to in central government against wider societal challenges and contexts. Given the complexity of what was discussed the tone could easily have drifted into pessimism, but I left the event with a sense of optimism that many people with power and influence are engaging on these topics, particularly the place of the UK in a changing world and how digital platforms are affecting personal relations. The connecting thread across the day was that a country that isn't a good place to live doesn't attract the people it needs, whether that's researchers, investment or workers, and doesn't generate the connection between people that growth depends on. Isolation, soft power and growth turn out to be the same problem viewed from different angles, which is a framing I haven't often seen made explicit in policy conversations.
