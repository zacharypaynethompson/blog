Picture this. You're in Málaga. You've had four beers, you've eaten four types of fried potato, some tortilla, a plate of croquetas, and you've just decided to also have some fried chorizo. It's your third day. You're dehydrated from the sun and slightly burnt because you didn't put sun cream on. You think, maybe I need my first vegetable of the holiday.

So you look for the salads. This is part of why British people love Spanish food, the fried fish and the sausage, but at some point the body asks for something green. You see *ensaladilla rusa*, the waiter says it's the most popular one, and it arrives, and it's about 50% mayonnaise. This is not what you were looking for in a salad. You were looking for the colours of the Spanish flag, some tomatoes, maybe a bit of lettuce. You got a bowl of mayonnaise with potato in it.

That's the thing I keep coming back to. In Spain the mayonnaise-based salad is the popular one, the one the waiter recommends. It's a serious food. Across a lot of Europe, *ensaladilla rusa* (or Olivier salad, or whatever the local name is) is what you put on the table at Christmas and New Year.[^1] Here, the equivalent is coleslaw, and coleslaw is a joke.

## the mayonnaise identity

I think British people are unusually likely to hold "I don't like mayonnaise" as an actual part of their identity. Not indifference, more of a small stated position, the kind of thing you'd put on a dating profile.

My girlfriend is the purest case of this I know. She won't eat mayonnaise. She hates it enough that she can't make her own tuna mayo mix, so I have to make it for her. She then eats the tuna mayo mix, and refuses to accept that it has mayonnaise in it. She dislikes it that much.

But she will happily eat aioli. This is the bit I find genuinely funny, because traditional Catalan allioli is just garlic and oil, no egg, but the aioli you actually get in a restaurant these days is usually made with egg, which makes it, functionally, garlic mayonnaise.[^2] You'll notice a British "tapas" menu often quietly drops the *ensaladilla rusa* but keeps the aioli, because aioli doesn't sound like mayonnaise. It's the same border my girlfriend polices at home. The recipe doesn't respect the border at all.

## where we let mayonnaise live

So it's not that we don't eat it. It's that we've decided where it's allowed to go, and it's only allowed in the unserious places.

Coleslaw is the main one, and I say this as someone who likes coleslaw, our coleslaw isn't even very good. A slaw from a Caribbean place is a much nicer thing, still mayonnaise-based, but treated with some care. Ours is the beige tub next to the thing you actually ordered. There is, as far as I can tell, no Coleslaw Appreciation Society.

France has one for egg mayonnaise. It's called the Association pour la Sauvegarde de l'Œuf Mayonnaise, it was started by a food critic, and it runs an annual world championship where bistros compete to serve the best *œuf mayo*.[^3] It's a hard-boiled egg with mayonnaise on it. In France that has a governing body and a trophy. Here the equivalent is the thing that comes with a jacket potato when you didn't ask for it.

## I actually love this stuff

Here's my confession. I really like all of it.

Marie rose sauce, which is mayonnaise and ketchup (sometimes salad cream and ketchup, but usually mayonnaise), tastes like Christmas to me. I have prawn cocktail as a starter every Christmas and it's one of my favourite parts of the day. Coronation chicken is definitely mayonnaise, and I have a coronation chicken sandwich almost every day, and I think it's great. None of this is glamorous. I'm aware that admitting to a daily coronation chicken sandwich is not a flex. But it's genuinely good, and I don't understand why I'm supposed to be a bit sheepish about it.

## the salad cream yorkshireman

The person who embodies the whole thing for me is a man I met who'd been living as an expat in Portugal, from Yorkshire, who drove his van all the way back to the UK and stocked up on two things before the return leg: salad cream, and Celebrations, from which he ate only the Maltesers.

I love this because it's such steadfast devotion to a condiment I could personally never eat again and be completely fine. Salad cream is strange to me. It's a bit thick, the name is too close to sun cream, and I'm not sure what you're meant to eat it with. It turns out it was invented by Heinz in 1914, the first product they ever made specifically for the British market, and it isn't really mayonnaise, it has much less oil and a lot more vinegar.[^4] So it's a British thing, made for Britain, that we then largely decided we were a bit embarrassed by. A man drove it across a continent in a van. I don't know where that leaves us.

## why, though

I don't really know why the disdain is there, and I don't want to pretend I do. But I have a suspicion.

Is it that the French love mayonnaise, and the Belgians love mayonnaise, and somewhere in the national psyche we've decided that's a bit much, a bit soft, a bit continental, and we don't want to be like that? It would fit a pattern. A lot of British identity is defined against the people across the channel who take their food seriously, and this could just be another instance of it. Do we hold "I don't like mayonnaise" the way we hold other small refusals to be like the neighbours? I genuinely can't tell, and I'd be overclaiming if I said it was that.

What I do know is that we eat it, some of us really like it, and we've somehow been told it doesn't count. We don't need a National Coleslaw Day, and we don't need a British version of the egg mayonnaise championship. But the fact that the idea sounds completely absurd here and completely normal in France is, I think, the whole thing. It's the same instinct I keep running into with [British food generally](/blog/posts/british-food-is-what-british-people-eat/), a thing we actually eat, and quietly like, that we've decided isn't worth taking seriously.

[^1]: *Ensaladilla rusa* is served as a tapa across Spain and is a Christmas and New Year staple in Russia and much of Eastern Europe (as Olivier salad), Poland, Serbia, and parts of Latin America. See [Russian salad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_salad).
[^2]: Traditional Catalan *allioli* and Provençal *aïoli* are an emulsion of garlic and olive oil with no egg, but modern recipes, and most restaurant and commercial versions, use egg or egg yolk as the emulsifier, making them effectively a garlic mayonnaise. See [Aioli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aioli).
[^3]: The [Association pour la Sauvegarde de l'Œuf Mayonnaise](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_pour_la_sauvegarde_de_l%27%C5%93uf_mayonnaise) (ASOM) was founded by the food critic Claude Lebey. Its motto is *le temps passe et les œufs durs* and it awards bistros that properly uphold the dish, including through a world championship.
[^4]: Salad cream was created by Heinz in 1914 and was the first Heinz product developed exclusively for the UK market. It has a lower oil content than mayonnaise (roughly 25-50%) and uses vinegar and water as its main constituents, giving it a sharper, more acidic taste. See [Salad cream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salad_cream).
