So you're in Málaga. You've had four beers (those ones with the guy mopping his brow),[^5] you've eaten four types of fried potato, some tortilla, a plate of croquetas, and you've just decided to also have some fried chorizo. It's your third day. You're dehydrated from the sun and your skin has taken on a crispy shade of pink. You think, maybe... I need some vegetables?

So you look for the salads. This is of course part of why British people love Spanish food, all fried fish and sausage, but at some point the body asks for something green. You see *ensaladilla rusa*, the waiter says it's the most popular one, and it arrives, and it's about 50% mayonnaise. This is not what you were looking for in a salad. You were looking for something light, something green, maybe a bit of lettuce. This resembles the worst of school dinner sides...

In Spain, a mayonnaise-based salad is a popular one, the one the waiter recommends! It's a *serious* food. Across a lot of Europe, *ensaladilla rusa* (or whatever the local name is) is what you put on the table at Christmas and New Year.[^1] In the UK, the equivalent is perhaps coleslaw or your plain "potato salad", and does anyone take these seriously?

## the mayonnaise identity

I think British people are unusually likely to hold "I don't like mayonnaise" as an actual part of their identity. Not indifference, more of a small stated position, the kind of thing you'd put on a dating profile maybe.

My girlfriend is a very pure case of this. She won't eat mayonnaise. She hates it enough that she can't make her own tuna mayo mix, so I have to make it for her. She then, confusingly, eats the tuna mayo mix, and refuses to accept that it has mayonnaise in it. She dislikes the concept of mayonnaise that much.

But she will happily eat aioli - because that's not mayonnaise. Traditional Catalan allioli is just garlic and oil, no egg, but the aioli you actually get in a restaurant these days is usually made with egg, which makes it, functionally, garlic mayonnaise.[^2] You'll notice a British "tapas" menu often drops the *ensaladilla rusa* but keeps the aioli, because aioli doesn't sound like mayonnaise.

My theory is that my girlfriend is emblematic of a deeper anti-mayo trend in this country. British people do eat mayonnaise, but are they perhaps in some type of denial?

## where we let mayonnaise live

So it's not that we don't eat it. It's that we've decided where it's allowed to go, and it's only allowed in places often deemed unserious.

Coleslaw comes to mind initially, and I say this as someone who likes coleslaw, our coleslaw isn't even very good. A slaw from, say, a Caribbean takeaway is a much nicer thing, still mayonnaise-based, but feels treated with some care. Ours invokes images of the back of the reduced shelf, yellow sticker upon yellow sticker signifying its cultural disdain. There is, as far as I can tell, no Coleslaw Appreciation Society.

France, however, does have one - for egg mayonnaise. It's called the Association pour la Sauvegarde de l'Œuf Mayonnaise, and it runs an annual world championship where bistros compete to serve the best *œuf mayo*.[^3] This is a hard-boiled egg with mayonnaise on it and, in France, has a governing body and a trophy.

## I do love this stuff

I really like all of it.

Marie rose sauce, which is mayonnaise and ketchup (sometimes salad cream and ketchup, but usually mayonnaise), tastes like Christmas to me. I have prawn cocktail as a starter every Christmas and it's one of my favourite parts of the day. Coronation chicken is definitely mayonnaise, and I have a coronation chicken sandwich almost every day, and I think it's great. None of this is glamorous. I'm aware that admitting to a daily coronation chicken sandwich is not really a flex. But it's genuinely good, and I don't understand why I'm supposed to be a bit sheepish about it!

## the salad cream yorkshireman

The person who embodies this for me is a man I met who'd been living as an expat in Portugal, from Yorkshire, who drove his van all the way back to the UK and stocked up on two key things before the return leg: salad cream, and Celebrations (from which he ate only the Malteser ones).

This is a beautiful, steadfast devotion to a condiment that epitomises negative perceptions of British food. I can see why it holds its place in the public psyche, it's certainly too viscous to dress a salad and the name is a bit too close to "sun cream". It turns out it was invented by Heinz in 1914, the first product they ever made specifically for the British market, and it isn't really mayonnaise, it has much less oil and a lot more vinegar.[^4] So it's a British thing, made for Britain, that we've largely decided we're a bit embarrassed by. 

## why, though

I don't really know why the disdain is there, but I have a suspicion.

Is it that the French love mayonnaise, and the Belgians love mayonnaise, and somewhere in the national psyche we've decided that's a bit much, a bit soft, a bit continental, and we don't want to be like that? It would fit a pattern. A lot of British identity is defined against the people across the channel who take their food seriously, and this could just be another instance of it. Do we hold "I don't like mayonnaise" the way we hold other small refusals to be like the neighbours?

What I do know is that we eat it, some of us really like it, and we've somehow been told it doesn't count. We don't need a National Coleslaw Day, and we don't need a British version of the egg mayonnaise championship. But the fact that the idea sounds completely absurd here and completely normal in France is, I think, the whole thing. It's the same instinct I keep running into with [British food generally](/blog/posts/british-food-is-what-british-people-eat/), a thing we actually eat, and quietly like, that we've decided isn't worth taking seriously.

[^1]: *Ensaladilla rusa* is served as a tapa across Spain and is a Christmas and New Year staple in Russia and much of Eastern Europe (as Olivier salad), Poland, Serbia, and parts of Latin America. See [Russian salad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_salad).
[^2]: Traditional Catalan *allioli* and Provençal *aïoli* are an emulsion of garlic and olive oil with no egg, but modern recipes, and most restaurant and commercial versions, use egg or egg yolk as the emulsifier, making them effectively a garlic mayonnaise. See [Aioli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aioli).
[^3]: The [Association pour la Sauvegarde de l'Œuf Mayonnaise](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_pour_la_sauvegarde_de_l%27%C5%93uf_mayonnaise) (ASOM) was founded by the food critic Claude Lebey. Its motto is *le temps passe et les œufs durs* and it awards bistros that properly uphold the dish, including through a world championship.
[^4]: Salad cream was created by Heinz in 1914 and was the first Heinz product developed exclusively for the UK market. It has a lower oil content than mayonnaise (roughly 25-50%) and uses vinegar and water as its main constituents, giving it a sharper, more acidic taste. See [Salad cream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salad_cream).
[^5]: [Victoria](https://www.cervezavictoria.es/en), "La Cerveza de Málaga", whose label features a man mopping his brow in the heat.
